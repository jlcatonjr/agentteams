Cliff Notes: Chapter 18: The Agent Investigator

Part IV: Emergence and Abstraction

A summary of Chapter 18, written to match the text as it describes agentteams 1.1.

Thesis

The chapter argues that a governance system which checks repositories against known templates needs a partner that reads across repositories without any template. The abstraction reviewer of Chapter 17 works top-down, and the chapter says of it that "It cannot find what the rules do not mention." The agent investigator works bottom-up and reports what no rule anticipated. The chapter holds that "Both modes are necessary." It treats the investigator's findings as genuine discoveries at the level of the system, while it denies that the agent experiences discovery in the way Kirzner describes.

What the chapter covers

The chapter opens with the investigator's purpose block, written in the second person as an agent file. The investigator performs "bottom-up audits of agent documentation across all collector repositories" and delivers "actionable remediation instructions to each repository's orchestrator."

Section 1 sets out the two modes of analysis. Top-down analysis starts from a rule set, as linters and the abstraction reviewer do. Bottom-up analysis "starts without a rule set" and looks for unnamed patterns and uncovered inconsistencies. Each mode fails alone. Top-down governance alone becomes rigid, and bottom-up discovery alone becomes chaotic, so that "Findings are generated but never systematized." The chapter ties the bottom-up mode to Kirzner's entrepreneurial alertness and to Keyhani's contrast between optimizing inside a known space and discovering outside it. A stated scope limitation follows. The investigator covers only "documented assertions about agent behavior" and leaves other causes of behaviour to "separate behavioral-auditing mechanisms."

Section 2 defines three classes of findings. Class 1 findings are emergent common features. Class 2 findings are structural placement inconsistencies, which the chapter reads in Chapter 2's terms as proxies that "mislead rather than inform." Class 3 findings are scope creep. Each class has its own follow-up, namely evaluation for abstraction, remediation or refactoring. The chapter stresses that "Findings are not self-executing." Handing them to each orchestrator is a coordination problem of the kind Chapter 3 analyzes.

Section 3 describes the per-repo investigation report. Its worked example is a Class 2 finding that an agent file names a directory called strategies/ when the real directory is references/strategies/. The orchestrator needs only the signal and the fix, and the chapter calls the report "a sufficient statistic for action". The investigator "scans a dozen managed repositories in a single daily run." The chapter gives an illustrative tally of what a run "might produce" fifteen, eight and three findings by class. The daily pipeline archives the reports as institutional memory.

Section 4 argues that the investigator holds no private information, since "Every agent can read every file." Its contribution is "structured attention", an interpretive frame drawn from the cognitive-institutional nexus of Chapter 1. Caton (2017) supplies the claim that agents under search costs can be ecologically rational, and Caton (2019) extends the claim to creativity, which Class 1 findings exemplify.

Section 5 answers the objection that a scheduled script cannot discover anything. The chapter concedes that the investigator lacks "the felt surprise of human discovery." Following Vriend (2002), it moves the question to the system level. Its example is the finding that separate repositories had built "nearly identical error-handling patterns in their orchestrator files" that no template prescribed. With Mitchell (2006), it calls discovery "the emergent product of their interaction", meaning the fixed protocol and the evolving repositories. The section ends by handing the pipeline to Chapter 19.

Key concepts and mechanisms

  • Agent investigator. A workspace-level agent that audits agent documentation bottom-up across repositories and routes findings to each orchestrator.
  • Scope limitation. The investigator examines documentation only. Model behaviour, prompt ambiguity and context effects fall to other mechanisms.
  • Class 1, emergent common features. Patterns "that have independently appeared in multiple repositories but are not yet represented in the abstract layer."
  • Class 2, structural placement inconsistencies. Shared features or content placed in different locations across repositories.
  • Class 3, scope creep. "agent files that have absorbed responsibilities outside their defined role."
  • Class-to-action mapping. Class 1 triggers evaluation for abstraction, Class 2 triggers remediation and Class 3 triggers refactoring.
  • Per-repo investigation report. Classified findings with severity rankings, remediation instructions and cross-references to related findings in other repositories.
  • Structured attention. The protocol that turns plain text into evidence of "convergence, inconsistency, or drift."

Theoretical frames

Kirzner (1973) provides the model of entrepreneurial alertness, the capacity to notice what others have overlooked. The chapter later limits this to an analytical parallel that "must not be reified" as a claim about the agent's experience.

Keyhani (2019) supplies the contrast between optimization within a known solution space and discovery beyond it. In the chapter's mapping the reviewer optimizes and the investigator discovers.

Caton (2017) and Caton (2019) ground discovery and creativity in agent-based models that work under real constraints. The chapter concludes that "Discovery is a property of the agent-environment interaction" rather than of the agent's inner architecture.

Vriend (2002) relocates novelty to the system level, and Mitchell (2006) treats emergence as behaviour that cannot be deduced from components. Together they support the chapter's answer to the objection about scheduled scripts.

Why it matters

The chapter supplies the discovery half of the abstract layer. Without a bottom-up reader, the platform could enforce only what it already knows, and convergent patterns would go unnamed. The three finding classes give each discovery a route to action, either into the abstract layer or into local repair. The report format turns a complex scan into a signal that a local orchestrator can act on without repeating the scan.

The chapter is also careful about its own claims. It describes the investigator's work as "pattern-detection as system-level emergence, not entrepreneurial alertness in the sense of subjective discovery under uncertainty." That hedge lets the book use Kirzner as an analytical tool without attributing inner experience to software. The chapter sends the reader to its Chapter 26 discussion of limitations for the fuller caveat.
